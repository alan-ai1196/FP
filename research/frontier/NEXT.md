# Next mathematical attack

## What does a two-state stochastic predictor actually have to store?

Return to the asymmetric selector on independent fair signs, $f(a,b,c)=b$ when $a=+1$ and $c$ otherwise. The terminal outcome sign has mean $\beta f(a,b,c)$ with $0<\beta\le1/2$. Previously the optimal *deterministic* two-state reset read with one common confidence earned $H(\beta/2)$ against uniform. Allow arbitrary homogeneous stochastic transitions per raw sign and a separately calibrated terminal distribution for each state. Compare two physical observers. One learner stores and reports from the one binary state that was actually sampled. The other keeps the state probability and reports the exact mixture of the two readouts. Their reported log losses can differ even when their hidden transition law is identical.

An independent algebraic attack has proposed an upper bound for the sampled state with a polynomial certificate on the four transition probabilities. Verify it independently by exact Bernstein coefficients, direct enumeration of rational stochastic kernels and a separate entropy argument. Check that permits asymmetric output rows, personalized raw-name transitions with iid names conditional on each class, and an independent randomized reset. Specify which observer and probability arithmetic the argument needs.

For an explicit marginalized implementation, let the state keep its sign with probability $1/2$ and otherwise copy the new input, starting fair. Its state probability encodes all three input signs in eight dyadic values after reset. Test what happens when only the count of $M$ independently sampled replicas is retained and the scalar terminal confidence is learned. For $\beta=1/4$ and $1/2$, test $M=1,2,3,4,5$ and the exact mixture. Compare optimized log gains with the best one-bit sampled learner, certify any strict threshold by rational log bounds, and derive the weak-signal limit. Include the already established three-state deterministic exact selector at the same bit cost as any four-count scheme. These are computational-resource comparisons for an acquired prediction rule; its ordinary-data acquisition was tested separately.
