# Next mathematical attack

How much ordinary training data and installed parameter precision does a small recurrent predictor require when its possible source biases are unknown? A stopped evidence counter held total excess log loss below a fixed allowance with $\Theta(\log T)$ states for two known symmetric coins. The prototype values were supplied to that construction. Real learning must acquire them, and the cost of doing so could exceed the live memory. [Known-law theorem](FOUNDATION.md#how-much-memory-does-continued-prediction-require-for-two-noisy-coins) · [Enumeration and CPU check](EVIDENCE.md#checking-the-memory-needed-for-future-predictions)

## Mathematical target and prospective comparisons

Choose an unknown amplitude $\theta\in[5/8,7/8]$. Every episode draws a new fair, hidden orientation and emits independent bits of bias $\theta$ or $1-\theta$ conditional on that orientation. Admit $N$ independent two-bit calibration episodes from the same amplitude, without orientation labels. All communication into a fresh $T$-draw episode goes through one charged $B$-bit packet. During that episode permit arbitrary causal adaptation. Compare its cumulative expected log loss with a predictor that knows the amplitude and faces the same hidden fresh orientation. State the difference between a result uniform over the amplitude interval and one averaged under a named prior.

Derive separate lower bounds from the information in the calibration observations and from the size of the transmitted packet. Include a control receiving only independent singletons, a predictor which can learn from the reporting stream, and a fixed-precision output grid. Check whether the necessary exposures and packet size can be achieved simultaneously using only ordinary conditional prediction on paired observations and a bounded integer counter. Account for the distinction between the input-label alphabet, the stored parameter, the continuing state and arithmetic precision.

For a finite check, use horizons 64, 1,024 and 16,384, with $N=T/16,T,16T$. Compare the calibrated predictor against the true-amplitude counter, the full predictor with that same fitted amplitude, a half-count predictor re-estimating from the fresh stream, and a conventional Bayesian learner integrating the unknown amplitude. Report a price for the prototypes, their update rule and any temporary arithmetic used by an implementation. A finite matrix can test formulae and expose calibration or rounding losses, while a converse must cover more than the tested acquisition rules. The two-point family and the shape of a counter are supplied to this test. The emergence of those choices from unrestricted ordinary data remains open.
