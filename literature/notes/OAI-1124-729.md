## 1. Problem and main guarantee

The paper asks whether an explicitly supplied linear program can be solved with an arithmetic step count bounded by a polynomial in its numbers of variables and constraints, independent of the magnitudes and precision of its coefficients. Theorem 1 answers this affirmatively in a specified uniform exact-scalar model.

For real data \(A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\), \(b\in\mathbb R^m\), \(c\in\mathbb R^n\), maximize \(c^\top x\) subject to \(Ax\le b\). The algorithm returns one of the following certificates.

| Case | Returned certificate |
|---|---|
| Finite optimum | Primal \(x\), dual \(y\), slack \(s=b-Ax\), with \(s,y\ge0\), \(A^\top y=c\), \(s^\top y=0\) and \(s+y>0\) coordinatewise |
| Unbounded objective | A feasible \(x\) and a direction \(r\) with \(Ar\le0\), \(c^\top r>0\) |
| Infeasible | \(y\ge0\), \(A^\top y=0\), \(b^\top y<0\), with support at most \(1+\operatorname{rank}A\) |

Strict complementarity is an additional conclusion in the finite-optimum case. It is not merely an approximate solution or a decision about the sign of the optimum.

The historical discussion distinguishes bit-polynomial ellipsoid and interior-point algorithms from strong polynomiality. It also distinguishes the new algorithm from bounds depending on short matrix entries, circuit imbalance, condition measures or restricted matrix sparsity. The simultaneous rescaling obstruction illustrated in §1 is about preserving several circuit ratios at once. The companion polytope-diameter result concerns edge paths and is a separate manuscript, not a premise that all optimization algorithms must follow such paths.

## 2. Computational model and bit accounting

The program is uniform across dimensions and uses exact scalar addition, subtraction, multiplication, division by a nonzero scalar, comparisons and copying, with constants zero and one. Explicit strings, indices, generated rational descriptions and control operations pay their full bit cost. Sequential memory access can be used with polynomial overhead. The input is explicit, with dimensions and an end marker.

The number of these operations is polynomial in \(m,n\). On rational data, the bit lengths of intermediates are polynomial in the **complete binary input length**, denoted here by \(L\). Consequently a Turing simulation is polynomial in \(L\). The theorem does not make the cost of reading long coefficients disappear.

Section 3.4 handles two scalar conventions with pairs \((u,v)\), \(v>0\), denoting \(u/v\).

1. Direct scalar input begins as \((x,1)\). Rational bit bounds measure reduced representations of the pair's components.
2. Numerator and denominator input begins with integer scalars. Arithmetic may retain unreduced integer pairs, whose entire integer lengths remain polynomial in \(L\).

The scalar algorithm itself does not require fraction reduction. It uses ring operations and comparisons, apart from possible final division. Its log-height accounting bounds arithmetic formulas by the sum of the heights of their leaves.

Repeated unreduced elimination is avoided when computing determinants. The algorithm clears denominators by a product, computes a factorial multiple of a determinant through division-free Newton identities and uses bordered minors and Cramer's rule for rank, bases and solving. Support restrictions are recomputed from the original input plus current zero-coordinate constraints. This prevents a long chain of previously enlarged representations from becoming the next algebraic input.

The conclusions transfer to arbitrary ordered fields through the uniform bounded program, real closure and ordered-ring formulas. This is an algebraic statement about the given exact scalar operations.

## 3. Side decisions and witness reconstruction

Section 2 homogenizes feasibility through

\[
C=\{(\alpha b-Ax,\alpha)\},\qquad
C^\perp=\{(y,-b^\top y):A^\top y=0\}.
\]

For a coordinate, exactly one side has a nonnegative witness positive there. Existence follows from a theorem of alternatives. Orthogonality prevents simultaneous positive witnesses on opposing sides.

A nonnegative witness of minimal support at the desired coordinate is a circuit, meaning a nonzero vector with minimal support in the relevant subspace. If there were another direction supported there and zero at the designated coordinate, moving to the first vanishing positive coordinate would contradict minimality.

Introduce slots \((i,h)\) with \(h\in\{-1,+1\}\). A positive reciprocal diagonal scaling acts by \(2^{-hp_i}\). It preserves orthogonality between the two sides. A circuit coordinate becomes visible after scaling when it is a substantial fraction of the largest entry. The ideal example in Figure 1 turns \((1/16,1)\) into \((1,1)\), while scaling the dual coordinates reciprocally.

The actual algorithm combines three ingredients:

- Online compression supplies short substitutes for logarithms of original minors.
- Calibration produces a short rational basis approximation at queried prices.
- An extremal-prices procedure turns labels forced only at largest circuit coordinates into the side of every coordinate.

After the side decisions, repeatedly try deleting coordinates other than the desired one. Retain a deletion precisely when the required witness remains possible. The final restricted support spans a line, so ordinary linear algebra recovers the witness on the original scale.

Homogenization gives the sparse Farkas alternative. An improving ray is tested explicitly. In the finite case, an explicit mixed strict/weak linear system asks for primal-dual feasibility, objective equality and strict complementarity. No optimal objective value is assumed known when forming this system.

## 4. Common calibration, including unqueried circuits

Theorem 3 takes an ordered basis \(B_0\) of the paired-slot space and a precision parameter \(P\). A price query returns a rational basis approximation \(B\), or reports a restart. During any uninterrupted run, every circuit has one common assignment of positive integer demands bounded by

\[
R=2^{\operatorname{poly}(N,P)}.
\]

For each query, there exist a reciprocal scaling \(D\) and a lower triangular matrix \(L\) with positive diagonal such that

\[
B_*=LB_0D,\qquad
\|B-B_*\|_{\max}\le2^{-P}.
\]

Entries of \(B_*\) have magnitude at most two. Every sup-norm-one vector in the scaled space has basis coefficients bounded by \(H=2^{100N^2}\). Every circuit slot maximizing its assigned surplus has magnitude at least one third of that circuit's scaled sup norm. The same demands work across the uninterrupted run. There are only polynomially many restarts.

The scaling, all circuit demands and \(B_*\) are analytical witnesses. The algorithm does not output an exponentially large list of circuits or explicitly form an enormous scaling factor.

### Why the same minors govern both tasks

Fix earlier pivot columns and append one varying column. The resulting determinants form a cofactor row in the span of an initial segment of \(B_0\). These rows perform elimination. Taking independent columns outside a circuit similarly represents that circuit by cofactors. Thus normalized cofactor magnitudes control both basis construction and visibility of every circuit.

For parameters \(y\), write \(y\sim_M y'\) when all integer affine forms of coefficient \(1\)-norm at most \(M\) have the same signs, including zero. The projection lemma shows that feasibility of a bounded-coefficient system in \(k\) unknowns is preserved when parameters agree up to \(M=(k+1)A^{k+1}\). The proof uses Farkas multipliers on extreme rays, small supports and determinant bounds. Strict inequalities are treated with a common positive margin.

The common-lift argument imposes rounded constraints on all normalized cofactor log ratios. Very small ratios need only remain below a cutoff. The resulting coefficient bounds are independent of original price magnitudes. Projecting the system transfers feasibility from short proxies to a small perturbation of the true logs. One lifted price vector therefore works for all cofactor rows, including those never queried. This system is used for existence, not enumerated by the algorithm.

Pivoting on proxy maxima and preserving determinant signs produces the ordered basis approximation. Demands are a common shifted floor of the proxy logs. Rounding loses less than one, giving the one-third visibility margin.

## 5. Online logarithmic compression

Theorem 4 supplies individual rational proxy answers \(f_u\) for nonzero minor indices. Between restarts they extend to one short rational assignment on **all** minor indices, with

\[
f\sim_{2^K}g,\qquad \|g-\ell\|_\infty\le2^{-K},
\]

where \(\ell_u\) is the true base-two log magnitude. The precision value \(K\), not merely its encoding length, counts as additional size.

The proof has four parts.

**Bound independent logs.** A dissociated set has all subset sums of proxy logs separated by more than two. If it had \(D=(d+1)^6\) elements, the corresponding \(2^D\) products of minors would grow by factors above two. All \(2^{2^D}\) subset sums of those products would be distinct. Yet those sums are bounded-degree integer polynomials in at most \(d^2\) input entries with bounded coefficients. Counting these polynomials contradicts that many distinct sums. The argument bounds the number of scales, without bounding the absolute values of all input coefficients.

**Extend partial answers.** A maximal dissociated set expresses other logs to within a bounded offset as signed subset sums. Round offsets to a fixed grid and project the resulting affine sign conditions. Agreement at a larger coefficient bound on the already answered indices yields an extension on every index. The maximal set is an existence device and need not be found exhaustively.

**Extract common scales and answer requests.** A Frank–Tardos style simultaneous approximation step, using LLL, represents the maintained log vector by at most its dimension many scales with bounded integer digit vectors. A request seeks a unique short digit sequence at those scales. Failure adds the requested minor to the maintained dissociated set and causes a restart. Hence at most \(D-1\) restarts occur. Success replaces true scales by large integer powers and rounds the final residual, preserving bounded affine signs through the first nonzero digit.

**Implement log sign tests without a free logarithm oracle.** Small logs are approximated by controlled series and rounded to explicit bits. Large logs can be retained implicitly through their minors. An affine comparison then becomes a product-of-powers comparison, performed by binary powering. In the branch requiring large powers, a large minor log itself certifies that the rational input length is sufficient to bound intermediate height. Each query starts from original matrix data and short discrete information.

The generalization at the end of §5 applies to bounded-degree, bounded-coefficient integer-polynomial families in a bounded number of scalar inputs, when individual evaluations have the specified implementation. It is not a theorem about arbitrary unrelated real measurements.

## 6. From extremal labels to satisfying all demands

Theorem 2 is an abstract deterministic oracle theorem. A hypothesis consists of a nonempty slot set and integer demands \(a_s\in[-R,R]\). At prices \(p\), its surplus is \(a_s-hp_i\). Consistency requires that **every** maximum-surplus slot receive its sign whenever the maximum is nonnegative. Other coordinates may receive arbitrary labels.

The algorithm returns prices satisfying every demand of every hypothesis consistent with its entire query transcript, in bit time polynomial in \(N,\log(R+1)\). It need not identify one unique hidden hypothesis. The label condition is essential.

### Market, amplification and recursive investment

An expert bids on slot options with utility bid minus price, plus an outside option of zero. Prices minimize the wealth-weighted sum of maximum utilities. Pay the least gain among tied maximizing options. The directional derivative at a minimizing price shows that total weighted gain is nonpositive for every feedback vector. Multiplicative updates preserve positive wealth without increasing total wealth.

One expert per full hypothesis proves a short query bound but would cost exponential time to maintain. The actual algorithm uses experts guessing four slots and their demands, together with a possible threshold pivot and level.

A recursive child returns prices \(x\). The parent's feedback at proposal \(y\) is the sign of \(x_i-y_i\), using the queried label at \(x\) when they coincide. A successful child gives correct feedback on every slot above its assigned threshold.

The proof tracks a distribution on a fixed consistent hypothesis's slots. Light probes violate the current threshold on mass at most \(1/5\). Heavy probes raise the child's threshold to a pivot in the quantile band \([3/16,1/5)\). Mass above the cutoff is boosted by four before normalization. At logarithmic depth, the coordinatewise median of price history must satisfy the leaf threshold, or a repeatedly violated slot would acquire mass exceeding one.

Four independent sampled slots hit the guaranteed-feedback mass with probability \(1-(1-3/16)^4>1/2\). This produces a positive expected log gain on a heavy successful probe. To avoid logarithmic branching at every level, a parent invests a small share of every expert's wealth in its child. If the child fails, its large returns, averaged with suitable reserved future-pivot weights, cover the parent's possible feedback losses. Total wealth cannot increase, so the root cannot fail.

The displayed parameters are \(c=2^{-14}\), \(r=2^{19}+1\), with a larger logarithmic number of root probes. The proof bounds the number of probes by \(O(N^{60}\log(NR+1))\). This is a polynomial existence bound with large constants and degree, not an implemented practical solver.

### Implicit expert pool

Demand guesses form integer boxes of dimension at most five. Comparisons involve individual guesses and pairwise differences. Refining their interval partitions yields polynomially many cells, each sharing maximizing options, gains and wealth. Fixed-dimensional elimination computes cell counts and sums of guesses.

A separation oracle for the market epigraph is built from those counts. Ordinary bit-polynomial ellipsoid optimization returns a minimizing vertex. Price ties imply half-integral prices of bounded magnitude through the tree. Lexicographic minimization makes the procedure deterministic.

Both the market LP and the later label LP operate on short rational data. Using an existing bit-polynomial LP subroutine here does not assume the strong-polynomial conclusion.

## 7. Turning calibrated bases into labels

For linear programming, let \(Q(y)=\sum_i y_{i,+}y_{i,-}\). It vanishes on the exact paired space and remains invariant under reciprocal scaling. With \(\epsilon=H^{-10}\) and basis error \(H^{-100}\), consider bounded row combinations of \(B\) that are almost nonnegative and coordinatewise bounded above.

Each normalized nonnegative circuit is approximated inside this convex candidate set. Its visible slot reaches the test threshold \(1/4\), below the true \(1/3\) visibility bound. All candidates have complementarity gap at most \(H^{-1}\).

Opposing slots cannot both reach \(1/4\), even in different candidates. Their midpoint would have both paired coordinates bounded positively away from zero, contradicting the small gap once the tiny possible negative entries are accounted for. This produces consistent labels. Positive demands then make the signs of final prices identify all sides. Together with witness recovery, this completes Theorem 1.

## 8. Complementarity, quadratic optimization and conic scope

Theorem 5 treats \(z,w\ge0\), \(w=Mz+q\), \(z^\top w=0\), assuming \(M+M^\top\succeq0\). It returns a solution or \(u\ge0\), \(M^\top u\le0\), \(q^\top u<0\).

Monotonicity has two roles. First, feasibility of the linear inequalities implies existence of a complementary solution. The proof minimizes the convex quadratic gap and uses stationarity to express zero as a sum of nonnegative terms. Second, it gives a convex candidate set for the label test.

The ordered calibration basis preserves the subspace with homogenizing parameter zero and the positive orientation of the final coefficient. In row coefficients \((v,\lambda)\),

\[
Q(\xi B_*)=v^\top A_*v-\lambda g_*(\xi),\qquad A_*\succeq0.
\]

A rotated-cone inequality includes every scaled solution. Small positive shifts and a PSD correction make its rational approximation stable even when \(\lambda\) is tiny. A perspective transform reduces the short-data label decision to ordinary bit-polynomial convex quadratic programming. The same midpoint exclusion identifies zeros retaining a solution circuit. A final LP on original data recovers the solution.

Corollary 6 obtains affine variational inequalities over explicit polyhedra and convex QP with symmetric PSD quadratic part. It returns an optimum and multipliers, a Farkas infeasibility certificate, or a feasible ray whose direction satisfies

\[
Ad\le0,\qquad Gd=0,\qquad c^\top d<0.
\]

The paper also gives a linear description of the monotone LCP solution set after one solution is known, and obtains maximum support by linear feasibility and averaging.

Corollary 7 concerns one Lorentz constraint plus linear inequalities, or multiple Lorentz constraints whose quadratic Hessians span at most one dimension. It is **not** a general strong-polynomial SOCP theorem. The multiple-constraint extension may need an exact output \(a+b\sqrt d\). The example forcing \(x=\sqrt2\) explains why the representation matters.

## 9. Contractions and exact piecewise-affine fixed points

Theorem 8 assumes a sup-norm contraction of the cube with Lipschitz constant at most \(1-2^{-b}\). Its oracle accepts a dyadic point and precision \(m\), returning a dyadic approximation with error at most \(2^{-m}\). Dimensions and precision parameters are unary.

It finds a point within \(2^{-k}\) of the unique fixed point in bit time polynomial in \(n,b,k\), including every query and answer bit. For a nonexpansive map, it instead guarantees residual at most \(2^{-k}\) in polynomial \(n,k\) time. A residual guarantee in the latter case does not identify distance to a unique fixed point.

The fixed point defines rounded demands on both signs of each coordinate. A maximum-surplus coordinate is close enough to a maximum-error coordinate that contraction forces its movement toward the fixed point by more than the evaluation uncertainty. This gives the extremal-label consistency. Slightly shrinking a nonexpansive map reduces the residual problem to contraction.

Corollary 9 concerns explicit rational circuits of affine, minimum and maximum gates. Sufficiently small residual places the output in a selected closed cell containing an exact fixed point, by rational LP denominator bounds. A final cell LP recovers the exact point. The total-search version can instead return a range violation or a pair violating nonexpansiveness. Recorded evaluations passing these checks admit a coordinatewise McShane extension, which justifies the time and residual guarantee even when the supplied circuit lacks the promise.

Proposition 10 allows rational polynomial slopes and arbitrary real polynomial offsets with a vanishing parameter. Determinant coefficients are linear in offsets because offsets occupy only the right-hand-side column. Frank–Tardos rounding preserves their bounded-coefficient signs. A short positive parameter value selects a stable outcome, which is transferred back through Cramer's rule and interpolation. Output is a rational function valid for all sufficiently small positive parameters. Polynomial degrees and slope encoding lengths remain charged.

## 10. Games and tropical feasibility

Theorem 11 concerns **explicit finite turn-based perfect-information arenas**. It computes exact values and pure positional policies guaranteeing them against arbitrary history-dependent opposing strategies.

| Setting | Step bound and qualifications |
|---|---|
| Deterministic two-player discounted or priority mean-payoff game | Strongly polynomial in the explicit arena and discrete data, with arbitrary real rewards, weights and discounts |
| Two-player stochastic version | Polynomial with binary rational transition-probability lengths included |
| One-player MDP | Strongly polynomial even for arbitrary real transition probabilities supplied as scalars |
| All-rational input in these settings | Polynomial intermediate/output bit lengths and polynomial bit complexity |
| Discounted simultaneous-move games discussed in §9.1 | Polynomial-bit approximation of values, not the same exact positional-policy theorem |

Ordinary mean payoff is a special case of priority mean payoff. Least infinitely recurring priority determines which weighted rewards enter the latter. Reachability and parity win probabilities follow by payoff transformations. Priority mean payoff is distinguished from the separate mean-payoff-parity objective.

The proof first handles short slopes by exact piecewise-affine fixed points. Setting \(\beta_e=1-w_et^{q_e}\), with reward \((1-\beta_e)r_e\), produces Blackwell-optimal policies. Gimbert–Zielonka's transfer theorem relates their small-\(t\) limit to priority mean payoff. An incoming-outcome state expansion reconciles the source theorem's state-based data with this paper's outcome-based convention.

For MDPs, LP evaluates policies and finds optimal values. Polynomial parameter data are handled by linear coefficient systems and successive positivity tests on lowest nonzero coefficients. For two players with arbitrary discounts, best-response MDPs give lower and upper policy guarantees. A clipped nonexpansive update propagates corrections using undiscounted transition rows while placing discounts in offsets. A constant number of these updates produces policies whose guarantee width halves.

Exact termination requires more than convergence. Every positive policy error is within a controlled multiplicative factor of one of polynomially many scales. The proof uses action gaps, reachability, stationary distributions with short rational coefficients, and linear independence of well-separated combinations. Only discrete policies pass between iterations. This preserves degree and bit bounds even for the symbolic vanishing-parameter computation.

Corollary 12 solves explicitly given tropical linear feasibility and returns a solution finite at every coordinate that can be finite in any solution. The reduction uses a bipartite mean-payoff game. An optimal policy with no reachable negative cycle gives finite shortest-walk potentials. This is a specific tropical representation result, not an unrestricted claim about compressed constraint systems.

- Can all predicates in a strongly polynomial LP algorithm have degree polynomial in the dimensions? Some compression comparisons here have exponential degree.
- What stronger exact results hold for separation-oracle descriptions, where geometric parameters still appear?
- Can two-player stochastic game complexity cease to depend on transition-probability encoding lengths?

## 12. Applicability boundary for the requested research

The result can serve as a working premise for an **explicit finite LP with its variables, constraints and exact coefficients actually supplied**. Any application must account for the size and construction of that LP. A formulation with exponentially many latent states or constraints does not become polynomial in the compact description simply by applying Theorem 1. The paper does not remove the cost of acquiring or estimating coefficients.

Its online compression concerns correlated minor-polynomial evaluations from a known matrix. Its learning procedure assumes extremal-label consistency at adaptive queries. Neither is a statistical theorem for recovering an unknown channel or an unobserved joint distribution from disjoint noisy panels. Its games assume an explicit known arena, and its fixed-point oracle assumes specified precision access.

No new FP theorem, state-selection guarantee, finite-report bound or latent-capacity identification result is derived in this note. Any such use requires a concrete reduction with the explicit dimension, access and bit assumptions retained. The requested four-paper reading is complete, and ordinary reading can resume subject to new Astra requests.
