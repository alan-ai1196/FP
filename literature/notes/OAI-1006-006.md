## 1. Question, result and stated consequences

The paper claims a fixed zero-free half-plane
\[
\operatorname{Re}s>11/12
\]
for every finite-order Hecke L-function over \(K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})\), and hence for every Dirichlet L-function, including zeta. Principal poles are allowed. The region is independent of conductor and height; the estimate constants used to prove it can depend on the fixed target character. The boundary itself is not included.

The paper describes this as a simpler intermediate result toward the \(7/8\) theorem in the separately published September 30 manuscript. Its proof is self-contained relative to its named external analytic-number-theory inputs; it does not simply invoke that stronger theorem. It also does not claim RH or GRH.

The introduction states the following consequences, using cited standard arguments or computations:

- An effective absolute bound \(O(x^{11/12}\log x)\) for the error in counting primes in reduced residue classes, uniformly over \(q\le x\).
- A fixed-power-of-\(\log p\) bound for the least quadratic nonresidue, and therefore deterministic square-root extraction in time polynomial in \(\log p\).
- A deterministic polynomial-time implementation of Miller's primality test, while explicitly noting that AKS already gave unconditional polynomial-time primality testing.
- An effective lower bound \(h(D)\gg\sqrt{|D|}/\log\log|D|\) for negative fundamental discriminants.
- Exclusion of Landau–Siegel zeros in the stated sense, and, together with cited class-group computations and the reduction to fundamental discriminants, completeness of Euler's 65 idoneal numbers.

These are recorded as source claims with their imported dependencies, not as separately re-proved consequences in this reading. The paper has no empirical experiment or benchmark. [§1.]

## 2. The central mean-square theorem

Let \(\mathcal O=\mathbb Z[\omega]\), \(N(a)=|a|^2\), and let \(S\) contain primes above 2 and 3 and the conductor of a fixed finite-order Hecke character \(\nu\). Ideals prime to 3 are represented by their unique primary generators. The sextic symbol \(\chi_n(u)=(u/n)_6\) is zero when \(u\) and \(n\) are not coprime.

For a smooth compactly supported test \(W\), define
\[
A_u(D)=\sum_{(n,S)=1}\mu(n)\nu(n)\chi_n(u)W(N(n)/D).
\]
The main analytic proposition asserts that, for each fixed \(0<\vartheta\le1/10\) and every \(\varepsilon>0\), a finite derivative order \(k(\vartheta,\varepsilon)\) gives
\[
\sum_{0<N(u)\le D^{1+\vartheta}}|A_u(D)|^2
\ll \|W\|_{C^k}^2D^{2+\vartheta+\varepsilon}.
\]
The implied constant can depend on \(\nu,S\), the fixed support interval, \(\vartheta,\varepsilon\). The theorem is not stated at \(\vartheta=0\), nor for arbitrary coefficients replacing \(\mu(n)\nu(n)\).

The six-power rows extract the original Möbius sum. For prime ideals with \(Y/2<N(p)\le Y\),
\[
\chi_n(p^6)=\mathbf1_{p\nmid n},\qquad
A_{p^6}(D)=A_1(D)+O_W(D/Y).
\]
The prime ideal theorem supplies \(\asymp Y/\log Y\) distinct rows. With \(Y=D^{(1+\vartheta)/6}\),
\[
|A_1(D)|^2
\ll D^{11/6+5\vartheta/6+\varepsilon}
   +D^{5/3-\vartheta/3}.
\]
Choosing a sufficiently small positive \(\vartheta\) for each requested final loss yields \(A_1(D)\ll D^{11/12+\varepsilon}\). This order of choices avoids asserting an unavailable endpoint mean-square bound. [§§2–3.]

If \(L_K(\varrho,\nu)=0\) with \(\operatorname{Re}\varrho>11/12\), choose \(W(y)=y^{-\varrho}\phi(y)\), where \(\phi\ge0\) is a nonzero smooth function supported in \((1,2)\). Then \(\widehat W(\varrho)>0\). The Mellin integral of \(A_1(D)\) is holomorphic on the claimed half-plane and equals \(\widehat W(s)/L_K^S(s,\nu)\) initially for \(\operatorname{Re}s>1\). Continuation gives a contradiction at \(\varrho\). Omitted Euler factors are nonzero in this region.

Quadratic base change gives \(L_K(s,\chi\circ N)=L(s,\chi)L(s,\chi\chi_{-3})\), up to harmless Euler factors. Possible pole–zero cancellation at \(s=1\) is addressed by Dirichlet nonvanishing. [§3.]

## 3. Arithmetic conversion under Poisson summation

Set
\[
\alpha(n)=n/|n|,\qquad
a_\xi(n)=\overline{\alpha(n)}\gamma_2(n)\xi(n),
\]
where \(\gamma_j\) is a normalized sextic-character Gauss sum and \(\xi\) belongs to a fixed finite ray-character group. That group absorbs the original character and supplementary reciprocity factors; moving prime factors are handled explicitly.

The identities driving the two-way conversion are
\[
\gamma_2(n)^3=\mu(n)\alpha(n),\qquad
\gamma_1(n)\gamma_2(n)=\mu(n)\alpha(n)G(n),
\]
\[
\overline{\alpha(n)}\gamma_2(n)\gamma_1(n)=\mu(n)G(n),
\qquad
\mu(n)\gamma_{-1}(n)
=\chi_n(-1)G(n)^{-1}\overline{\alpha(n)}\gamma_2(n).
\]
For coprime squarefree \(a,b\),
\[
a_\xi(ab)=a_\xi(a)a_\xi(b)\chi_b(a)^4.
\]
The function \(G\) is a fixed-ray quadratic refinement, and the reciprocity factor \(\mathcal R\) is a symmetric sign-valued bicharacter. One must retain its quadratic refinement law rather than treat \(G\) as an ordinary multiplicative character.

The Poisson formula includes a row exclusion \((k,\mathfrak r)=1\). Inclusion–exclusion introduces divisors of \(\operatorname{rad}\mathfrak r\), with their norm factors and frequency scales. A primitive nonprincipal character kills zero frequency. For the primitive principal character, the exact zero term contains \(\prod_{p\mid\mathfrak r}(1-N(p)^{-1})\). Nonzero frequencies in diagonal sectors are retained. [§4.1–4.2.]

## 4. The canonical dual family and first reduction

The normalized dual energy is
\[
\mathcal E(\mathcal H,X,F;\xi,W)
=\frac1{XF}\sum_{\substack{F\le N(f)<2F}}^*
 \sum_{0<N(k)\le\mathcal H}
 \left|\sum_n^*a_\xi(n)\chi_n(k)\chi_n(f)^4W(N(n)/X)\right|^2.
\]
Stars mean squarefree primary indices prime to \(S\). The fourth-power auxiliary label \(f\) carries exact local information from common column factors.

An additional column exclusion is removed by Möbius inversion and the change
\[
(X,F)\longmapsto(X/N(d),FN(d)).
\]
This preserves \(XF\) and the row range. The fourth-power character supplies the necessary zero masks, and enlarging the injective image \(f\mapsto df\) is done only after a positive norm has been formed.

In the original mean square, write the two columns as \(gz_1,gz_2\), apply Poisson with exclusion \(g\), and remove the residual \((z_1,z_2)=1\) by a complete divisor sum. The bijection
\[
b=g/e,\quad f=ev,\quad k=eh,\qquad e=(f,k)
\]
produces this canonical family. With \(H=D^{1+\vartheta}\), its scales satisfy
\[
X=\frac D{BF},\qquad
\mathcal H\le\frac{CD^2}{HB^2},\qquad
\Sigma:=XF=\frac DB.
\]
The target is \(\mathcal E\ll D^\varepsilon\Sigma\), with finite smooth seminorm control. Kernels coupling both columns and depending on the row are handled by Appendix B's common-majorant lemma. [§4.3–4.4.]

## 5. Completion by cubes and finite descent

The cubic theta coefficient formula identifies \(a_\xi(n)\) with squarefree coefficients of a conjugate cubic theta series. Automorphy naturally applies to all indices \(nb^3\). The completed sum \(T(X;k,f)\) therefore includes cube factors with their exact multiplicative weights.

The completed mean-square proposition states
\[
\sum_{0<N(k)\ll\mathcal H}|T(X;k,f)|^2
\ll D^\varepsilon\|W\|_{C^J}^2
\left(\mathcal H+\frac{\mathcal H^2N(f)}X\right).
\]
It is insufficient to discard the cube terms: they can cancel the squarefree part.

Complete multiplicativity, including zeros, instead permits exact Möbius inversion in the cube variable. Split the inverse sum at
\[
H_c^3=\min\left(X,\frac{X^2}{\mathcal H^2}\right).
\]
The short part is within the target \(\Sigma=XF\). After expanding and regrouping the long part, weighted Cauchy–Schwarz leaves energies with column length \(L_b=X/N(b)^3\). Divisor weights cost logarithms. Lengths \(L_b\le1\) are handled by counting; when \(\mathcal H^2\le X\), no long nontrivial supremum remains.

The transfer proposition turns a remaining energy at length \(L\) into the same canonical type with
\[
\mathcal H'\le\frac{\mathcal HL}{\Sigma F},\qquad
\frac{\mathcal H'}{\Sigma'}\le\frac{\mathcal H}{\Sigma},
\qquad \Sigma'=X'F'\le L.
\]
It costs a controlled \(C^{4m+12}\) seminorm when the child estimate uses \(C^m\).

Under a fixed positive gap \(\mathcal H/\Sigma\le D^{-\kappa}\), the cube cutoff forces
\[
\mathcal H'<\mathcal H(\mathcal H/\Sigma)^2
\le D^{-2\kappa}\mathcal H.
\]
The proof inducts on bounded row-length bands, allocating epsilon losses across a finite number of steps. The derivative order may grow with the finite depth but is independent of the support interval; this permits the support enlargements in the transfer lemma. The terminal row range is bounded and is handled by counting.

The initial reduction has \(\mathcal H/\Sigma\ll D^{-\vartheta}\), so \(\kappa=\vartheta/2\) applies for sufficiently large \(D\). Bounded \(D\), short columns and empty rows are treated separately. This closes the central mean square. [§5.]

## 6. Why theta reflection yields a quadratic sieve

The completed coefficients are realized by finite additive translates of Kubota's cubic theta function. Their constant terms cancel. The reflected expression uses three fixed cusp coefficient sequences, with support
\[
\ell=u\lambda^mnb^3,\qquad m\ge-4,\quad n\ \text{squarefree},
\]
and coefficient bound \(27\cdot3^{m/6}|b|\).

At a moving prime, a character exponent \(j\) becomes
\[
B_{p,j}(x)=
\begin{cases}
\chi_p(x)^{-j-2},&j\ne0,4,\\
N(p)^{-1/2}(-1+N(p)\mathbf1_{p\mid x}),&j=4,\\
N(p)^{-1/2}\chi_p(x)^{-2},&j=0\text{ active}.
\end{cases}
\]
In particular \(j=1\) becomes quadratic. A zero exponent still means \(\mathbf1_{p\nmid x}\) before transformation. In the finite Fourier expansion it can also give an inactive branch, whose scalar is \(1-N(p)^{-1}\).

For squarefree rows, separate the primes supported on the auxiliary twist or fixed exclusions. In fixed ray classes, the cusp sequence and transformed additive character are independent of the residual row. This uniformity allows Goldmakher–Louvel's quadratic large sieve, with factor \(\mathcal H+U\), instead of a general higher-order sieve containing an additional \((\mathcal HU)^{2/3}\) term.

The \(j=4\) local factor is split into a small term and two divisibility terms assigned to the squarefree and cube variables. Reindexing shows how coefficient loss compensates conductor growth. The product of squared coefficient bound and transformed length is controlled by the stated auxiliary norm \(N(g)\).

Mellin separation preserves uniform row majorants. Dyadic column and cube sums converge using the transformed weight's decay. Finally write an arbitrary row as \(u_0sv^2\), with \(s\) squarefree and no coprimality assumption between \(s,v\). The identity
\[
T(X;u_0sv^2,f)=T(X;u_0s,fv^2)
\]
moves the square into the fourth-power auxiliary label. Summation of \(N(v)^{-2}\) converges and gives the completed estimate. [§6.]

## 7. Two Poisson transforms and the signed fiber calculation

The first transfer Poisson summation converts cubic Gauss coefficients back to Möbius coefficients. Removing residual column coprimality introduces \(C,t,d\); the family has columns with
\[
\mu(n)\xi_1(n)\mathbf1_{(n,t)=1}
\overline{\chi_n(y)}\chi_n(C)^4\chi_n(d).
\]
The old labels and frequencies map to \(y=hf^2\), with divisor-bounded multiplicity. The nonnegative row sum is enlarged by the factor \(\Sigma/(LF)\ge1\). This enlargement is what makes the second transform return a shorter row range.

The second Poisson transform restores cubic Gauss coefficients. Its canonical coordinates are
\[
r=tg/e,\qquad f'=Cew,\qquad k'=deh.
\]
All preimages with the same \(r,f',k'\) have the same kernel. Their signs must be combined before taking absolute values. At a prime dividing \(f'\), the three assignment types give
\[
(1+\mathbf1_{p\mid k'})
-\mathbf1_{p\mid k'}-\mathbf1_{p\nmid k'}
=\mathbf1_{p\mid k'}.
\]
Thus the regrouped sum has the exact condition \(f'\mid k'\).

That condition ensures the child column scale remains at least one even after removing the residual exclusion \(r\). Dyadic scales give
\[
X'=\frac L{RF'},\quad
\Sigma'=\frac LR,\quad
\mathcal H'=\frac{\mathcal HL}{\Sigma FR^2}.
\]
The normalization identity \((\Sigma R/L^2)R(L/R)^2=\Sigma\) accounts for summing the remaining \(r\)-labels. Zero frequencies contribute \(O(D^\varepsilon\Sigma)\), including cases where an intermediate positive scale is below one. Appendix B then separates the kernels, yielding the transfer proposition. [§7.]

## 8. The appendices and exact scope

Appendix A derives the Gauss–Jacobi identities, including the evaluation \(J(\chi_p^2,\chi_p^2)=-p\). A Gaussian-regularized Poisson calculation gives the quadratic Gauss factor as a four-class function modulo \(4\mathcal O\), with values \(1,1,i,-i\). This supplies the fixed-ray refinement and reciprocity bicharacter.

Its theta calculation treats reduced fixed denominators, including nonunit Fourier residues, and active moving primes separately. CRT choices place the cusp matrices in fixed residue classes. The local Fourier coefficient and the conjugated cubic automorphy multiplier together produce the exponent change \(-j-2\).

The archimedean calculation differentiates horizontally before Mellin transformation. Under cusp inversion, the derivative has no height-derivative term at the evaluation point. It kills constant modes, giving decay at both ends and an entire Mellin function. The resulting gamma quotient has numerator factors \(\Gamma(7/6+t)\Gamma(5/6+t)\); its first pole lies at \(-5/6\). The allowed contour shifts give
\[
|(x\partial_x)^jV_*^\sharp(x)|
\ll\|V_*\|_{C^J}\min(x^{1/4},x^{-A}).
\]
These statements include the fixed-ray uniformity needed in §6.

Appendix B proves three analytic tools: multivariable Mellin separation with finitely many derivatives; extension of common-test mean-square bounds to row-dependent smooth kernels; and weighted recombination. For the middle result, derivative order \(2m+4\) gives an integrable common Fourier majorant. It does not permit arbitrary uncontrolled row-dependent coefficients.
