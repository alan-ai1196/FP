## Question and observation model

Exact real measurements contain unlimited numerical precision. With all measurements retained, $d$ independent Gaussian equations identify a vector in $\mathbb R^d$ almost surely. The paper asks what changes when a one-pass learner carries only a finite state between those measurements.

Definition 1.1 fixes an unknown unit signal $s\in S^{d-1}$. The observations are

\[
x_t\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(0,I_d),\qquad y_t=\langle x_t,s\rangle.
\]

After every sample the persistent state has at most $2^M$ possible values. A transition may inspect the entire current real pair, perform unlimited computation and use fresh randomness. Its only retained information is the next finite state. The rules may depend on dimension, accuracy and sample index. A shared rule seed and initialization are jointly independent of the signal and all rows, although the seed and initialization may depend on each other.

Stopping is allowed before the first sample and at later indices, subject to a deterministic finite horizon $T$. The output is a unit vector generated from terminal state, stopping index and fresh randomness. It cannot use the discarded final pair. The rows are fresh, independent and fixed by the Gaussian sampling law. They cannot be chosen adaptively or revisited.

The experiment is jointly measurable. For each fixed seed, state and index, transition and stopping probabilities are Borel or measurable in the completion of $\gamma_1(dx)dy$. These conditions matter when replacing rules on null sets. The memory budget counts **bits rather than exact real registers**. [§1, pp. 2–4]

## Main conclusion and its quantifiers

For every fixed finite $A>0$, Theorem 1.2 gives constants $c_A>0,d_A$ such that, for $d\ge d_A$, $M\le Ad^2$ and $0<\epsilon\le1/10$,

\[
\Pr_{S\sim\sigma_d,\,X,\,\mathrm{randomness}}
\{\operatorname{angle}(\widehat S,S)\le\epsilon\}\ge\frac23
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
T\ge c_A d\log(1/\epsilon).
\]

The success premise averages over the uniform-sphere signal prior. A guarantee for every signal implies it by averaging the same learner. It does not assert that each individual signal is hard for each learner.

For $M$d$=o(d^2)$, applying the theorem eventually with $A=1$ gives an absolute positive constant. The eventual dimension threshold may depend on the memory sequence. Accuracy may vary arbitrarily within the stated interval, without a relation to memory. There is no positive constant claimed uniformly when $A=A$d$$ diverges. The lower bound is on the prescribed horizon, not on an arbitrary expected stopping time. [Corollaries 1.3–1.4, §6]

### Precision benchmark and comparisons

The displayed Kaczmarz update retains a real vector and satisfies

\[
z_t=z_{t-1}+\frac{y_t-\langle x_t,z_{t-1}\rangle}{\|x_t\|^2}x_t,
\qquad
\mathbb E\|z_t-s\|^2=(1-1/d)^t.
\]

This fresh-isotropic-row calculation explains the precision scale. It supplies **no matching finite-bit upper bound**. The cited Strohmer–Vershynin result uses sampling from a fixed matrix and is distinguished from this separate identity.

The paper compares Sharan–Sidford–Valiant's noisy Gaussian regression bound, whose cited v2 uses noise half-width $2^{-d/5}$, memory $d^2/4$, accuracy $d^{-r}$ and a particular range of $r$. A lower bound for noisy samples cannot establish the exact-sample theorem. In the opposite direction, an exact learner can inject the prescribed independent noise and simulate a noisy learner. Normalizing a successful Euclidean estimate at error $e\le1/20$ gives angular error at most $2e$. Consequently the new theorem implies an $\Omega_A(dr\log d)$ horizon lower bound in the cited noisy experiment, under the explicitly retained finite-state and measurability conventions.

The other comparisons separate per-message communication from total persistent memory, sparse noisy regression from this Gaussian law, discrete parity lower bounds from continuous labels, and approximate null-vector or empirical-residual tasks from fixed-signal estimation. These are comparisons reported in the source. Their full cited papers were not newly read. [§§1.1–1.2, pp. 4–5]

## Backward successful mass replaces a posterior argument

Conditioning on a raw exact observation makes the spherical signal posterior singular. The proof instead fixes the original prior and studies future success when the learner is started from an arbitrary specified state, including a state never reached by its actual run.

Set

\[
a=(d-1)/2,\qquad q=\lfloor(d-1)/8\rfloor.
\]

A continuation $h:S^{d-1}\to[0,1]$ has propagated scale $R$ if

\[
\int_{B(z,r)}h\,d\sigma_d\le(Rr)^a
\quad\text{for every }z\in S^{d-1},\ r>0.
\]

Here $R$ controls successful mass. It is not a posterior support radius.

For a terminal output law independent of the signal, sphere-centered caps have mass at most $r^{d-1}$, while all successful signals have mass at most $\epsilon^{d-1}$. Their minimum is at most $(\epsilon r)^a$. Thus every terminal law begins with $R=\epsilon$. The all-radius form is what the block induction preserves. [Lemma 2.1, pp. 6–7]

## Exact projection density and the geometric mechanism

Theorem 3.1 considers a finite, unnormalized nonnegative measure $\mu$, supported on an ambient ball $B(z,R_0)$, satisfying

\[
\mu(B(x,r))\le Hr^\beta
\quad\text{for every ambient }x,r.
\]

For an $m\times d$ standard Gaussian matrix $A$, the observed pair is $(A,As)$. Integers $m\ge1,p\ge2$ must satisfy $\beta>m+p-2$. With

\[
J_j=1+3^j2^\beta\frac{m}{\beta-j-m},
\]

the joint image has a Borel density $F$ relative to $\lambda_m(dA,dy)=\gamma_m(dA)dy$, and

\[
\|F\|_p\le
(2\pi)^{-m(p-1)/(2p)}
H R_0^{\beta-m(1-1/p)}
\left(\prod_{j=0}^{p-2}J_j\right)^{1/p}.
\]

The matrix remains part of the data. This is a bound on the joint matrix-label law, with no normalization by the measure's total mass. It also applies when $\mu$ has no density relative to spherical area.

The proof has three substantive steps. [§3, pp. 7–12]

1. A covering of an affine $j$-plane gives tube mass at most $3^j2^\beta H R_0^j t^{\beta-j}$. Integrating the tube estimate controls the inverse $m$-th power of distance. The strict exponent margin ensures integrability.
2. For independent copies $s_1,\ldots,s_p$, let $V=[s_2-s_1,\ldots,s_p-s_1]$. Successive orthogonalization writes the inverse Gram determinant as a product of inverse affine distances. Integrating from the last point backward retains all $p$ factors of the mass coefficient and the support-radius power.
3. Gaussian label smoothing expands the density moment into a determinant with covariance

\[
V^{\mathsf T}V+\delta^2(I+\mathbf1\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}).
\]

The common first noise contributes the rank-one term. The Gram estimate bounds the smoothed moment uniformly in $\delta$. Compactly supported tests converge to the exact-label measure, and $L^p$ duality yields its strong density bound. No hypothetical density for a reached-state posterior is used.

All localized continuations share the exact projected support

\[
\mathcal E_{z,r}=\{(A,y):y\in Az+A B(0,r)\}.
\]

Its average volume is

\[
\lambda_m(\mathcal E_{z,r})
=v_m r^m\mathbb E\sqrt{\det(AA^{\mathsf T})}
\le C_E^d r^m.
\]

The bound holds uniformly for short blocks as well as blocks of order $d$. [Lemma 3.7]

## Routing cost, local stopping indices and iteration

For a block of $m\le q$ rows and $N$ destinations, each routing function $g_j(A,y)$ is nonnegative and $\sum_jg_j\le1$. The route can inspect the entire block and use fresh randomness, a permission stronger than the original streaming interface.

Restrict destination $j$'s successful measure to $B(z,r)$, keeping it unnormalized. The projection theorem gives norm at most $e^{Cd}R^a r^{a-m(1-1/q)}$. Hölder on the common matrix-label domain and then on the finite destination set gives

\[
\int_{B(z,r)}h\,d\sigma_d
\le e^{Cd}N^{1/q}(Rr)^a.
\]

The projected-volume factor restores exactly the radius power lost by the density. The previous scale therefore obeys

\[
R'=R\exp(Cd/a)N^{1/(aq)}.
\]

Figure 1 on p. 13 depicts this cancellation. Its nodes are proof measures and bounds, not computational states of a learned model. [Lemma 4.1, §§3.3–4]

For an actual block the destinations comprise terminal states tagged with one of its $m+1$ local stopping offsets, plus continuing states at its end. Thus

\[
N\le(m+2)2^M.
\]

The boundary convention places the entrance stopping check inside the block and an active exit before the next check. A terminal tag at the final transition is distinguished from an active exit at that same index. This avoids charging a global $T$-dependent stopping alphabet. Every destination continuation starts with fresh future samples. [Lemma 5.1, pp. 14–15]

Completed-measurable rules are replaced by everywhere-defined Borel kernels for each fixed seed. The unconditional fresh-pair law under the chosen prior is dominated by $\gamma_1(dx)dy$. A pair falling into any modification null set is therefore null even when the current state depends on previous observations. This requires no reached-state conditional density. The replacement preserves the prior experiment, not behavior at every exceptional individual signal. Seedwise bounds are averaged through the original jointly measurable experiment, without assuming a jointly measurable choice of all replacements. [Lemma 5.2, pp. 15–17]

One block multiplies the scale by at most $\exp(C(1+M/d^2))$. Proposition 5.3 consequently gives

\[
P_{\rm succ}\le\min\!\left\{1,
\left[2\epsilon\exp\!\left(
C(1+M/d^2)\lceil T/q\rceil
\right)\right]^{(d-1)/2}\right\}.
\]

This finite success bound allows any $M\ge0$. Extracting the stated precision rate with a fixed positive constant uses $M\le Ad^2$.

## Completing the constant-accuracy endpoint

The rounded number of blocks leaves an additive loss when $\log(1/\epsilon)$ is bounded. The proof grants an estimator the full matrix and labels and reflects the unknown signal across the row span $U$. Signals $u+v$ and $u-v$ produce identical data. When $\|v\|>\epsilon$, one output cannot be close to both.

Using $\mathbb E\|P_US\|^2\le T/d$ yields the memory-independent estimate

\[
\Pr\{\|\widehat S-S\|\le\epsilon\}
\le\frac12+\frac{T}{2d(1-\epsilon^2)}.
\]

In particular, $T\le d/4$ and $\epsilon\le1/10$ imply success at most $62/99<2/3$. Combining this endpoint with the block bound proves the main theorem throughout its accuracy interval. The reflection argument uses rows independent of the uniform signal and a deterministic bound on their number. [Lemma 6.1 and §6, pp. 18–20]

Appendix D identifies the conditional residual sphere and sharpens the displayed endpoint to

\[
P_{\rm succ}\le\frac13+(2/5)^{3d/4-1}
\quad(T\le d/4)
\]

for sufficiently large $d$. It permits arbitrary randomized Euclidean outputs based on all data and explicitly includes the zero-row case. [pp. 39–40]

## Lower-dimensional cube and the appendix interfaces

Section 7 abstracts the selection step: a test bound $\int g\,d\eta_j\le U(\int_{\mathcal D}g\,d\lambda)^{1-1/p}$, common finite domain $\mathcal D$ and routes summing to at most one suffice to charge $N^{1/p}$. A strong density is sufficient but is not inferred merely from a setwise weak estimate.

Section 8 embeds $Q=[-1/2,1/2]^n$, $n=\lfloor d/2\rfloor$, into the unit sphere through paired coordinates $u_i/\sqrt n$ and $\sqrt{1-u_i^2}/\sqrt n$. The first coordinates transfer local parameter mass to ambient balls. The Lipschitz bound supplies a shared support ball. With $\alpha=n/4,\beta=3n/4,p=\lfloor n/4\rfloor$, the same projection and selection machinery yields

\[
P_{\rm succ,cube}\le
\min\{1,D^{n(b+1)}\epsilon^{n/4}\},
\qquad b=\lceil T/p\rceil,
\quad M\le d^2.
\]

Cube-average success alone gives the $d\log(1/\epsilon)$ lower bound at sufficiently fine accuracy. The stated all-accuracy consequence adds an every-signal guarantee, so the same learner also satisfies the uniform-sphere reflection endpoint. That extra quantifier must be retained. The cube prior is generally singular with respect to sphere area, illustrating why the arbitrary finite-measure theorem is useful. [pp. 21–26]

## Evidence, dependencies and limits

This manuscript is theoretical. It has no fitted model, dataset, empirical benchmark or measured hardware result. Its figure is a proof schematic. The principal construction is the successful-mass projection bound and its finite-state backward iteration.

The bibliography's main learning predecessors are Shamir, Steinhardt–Duchi, Steinhardt–Valiant–Wager, Raz, Sharan–Sidford–Valiant and Dagan–Kur–Shamir. Mattila and Drury provide geometric context. Fremlin, Tao, Hunter, the Stanford measure supplement and Gagné–Panangaden supply cited measure-theoretic ingredients. Their role was read from this manuscript. Their full texts and proofs were not recursively audited.

The theorem charges persistent memory while allowing unlimited within-transition computation and nonuniform rule descriptions. It does not price learning those descriptions, a read of a previous sample, an adaptive measurement, training time or real-register precision. A concrete extension to one of those interfaces would need its own argument. Its target is angular signal recovery under a specified prior, not predictive log loss or recovery of an unknown finite-state transition structure.

No substantive FP transfer is proposed. The source gives a precise finite-bit acquisition obstruction in its original Gaussian experiment. Applying it to ordinary token sequences would require an explicit reduction that preserves the signal prior, fresh-row interface, retained-state accounting and success event. Useful follow-up would be a finite-bit upper bound or a deliberately selected changed-observation theorem. Historical citation completion and official-theorem reproof are not queued by this note.
