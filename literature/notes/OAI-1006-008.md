## 1. Question and claimed answer

The input is an integral polynomial with its number of variables included in the input. The decision problem asks whether it has a rational zero. The paper claims that this problem is undecidable and has Turing degree `0′`.

The main construction is a **Turing reduction** from integer solvability. For a given nonconstant polynomial `f`, it effectively enumerates finite rational tests such that

\[
f\text{ has an integer zero}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{every finite test is rationally solvable}.
\]

A hypothetical rational-solvability oracle permits two searches in parallel: enumerate integer zeros and enumerate failing finite tests. One search must stop. This would decide integer solvability, contradicting the Davis–Putnam–Robinson–Matiyasevich theorem. Constant polynomials are handled separately.

The paper carefully separates this route from an existential definition of `Z` in `Q`, a Diophantine model of the integers, earlier universal definitions, and undecidability over large proper subrings or rings of integers. Its argument supplies no single existential definition of the integers. The discussion of Mazur-type obstructions concerns particular models and definability strategies, not an obstruction to every Turing reduction.

## 2. Finite tests and the additive index map (§2)

Fix

\[
F=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2),\qquad d=75-53\sqrt2,\qquad
E:y^2=x^3-d^2x.
\]

The rank-one calculation in §5 permits a fixed integer `m` and nontorsion point `P` with `mE(F)=ZP`. A recursively axiomatized ring carries points `T_a`, with `T_0=O`, `T_1=P` and `T_{a+a′}=T_a+T_{a′}`. Six ring-valued functions present the coordinates as fractions over the quadratic extension.

In an elementary extension of a fixed many-sorted arithmetic structure, the elliptic points give an injective additive map

\[
\eta:R\longrightarrow{}^*\mathbb Z,\qquad
\widetilde T_a=\eta(a)P,\qquad\eta(1)=1.
\]

Multiplicativity of `η` is **not** assumed. Establishing enough agreement between a ring element and its index is the purpose of the local tests.

### Paired local tests

For each fixed precision `K`, a finite polynomial `ℓ_K` truncates the formal elliptic logarithm in the parameter `z=−x/y`. At a good split prime, one test compares

\[
\ell_K(z(T_{ha}))/\ell_K(z(T_h))
\]

with the ring element `a`. A second test, on the conjugate curve at the same place, makes the ratio involving `T_{(h−4)a}^σ` and `T_{j_0}^σ` highly divisible. The same nonzero integers `h,j_0` serve every integer `a` at that occurrence and place.

The conjugate test yields `η(ha)≡4η(a)` modulo the desired prime power; applying it also to `a=1` makes `η(h)` a unit away from 2. The first test can then compare with `η(a)`. This pairing supplies integrality and `K`-adic congruence without assuming that the additive index map respects multiplication. Only finite polynomial identities and valuation assertions are transferred; no infinite formal-logarithm series is interpreted inside the nonstandard structure.

### Why the theory is recursive

The theory uses fixed finite algebraic data, finite lists of possible multipliers, and ordinary integer precision parameters. Localization membership is expressed by ring equations together with unit conditions. It is not an invocation of the entire true arithmetic of the integers as an algorithmic oracle.

After Skolemization, the tests enumerate ground ring/functionality constraints and instances of the existential pole condition. Auxiliary witnesses for those existential formulas and inverse variables for inequalities are not automatically added as new elements of the ring term system. Finite rational Boolean constraints become finitely many polynomial-solvability queries by clearing fixed denominators, replacing nonzero conditions by inverse equations, and using sums of squares for conjunctions over `Q`.

Compactness uses an ambient elementary diagram in the existence proof. That diagram is not the list of sentences the proposed decision procedure must query. Fixed arithmetic choices may be hard-coded independently of `f`; the contradiction needs existence of a single oracle machine, not an algorithm rediscovering the fixed geometry.

## 3. The pole-parity formula (§3)

The positive existential formula `Φ(b)` holds for every ordinary integer and, outside a fixed finite set of primes, prohibits **odd negative valuations**. It does not assert integrality and permits even-order poles.

Its witnesses involve finitely many multiplier choices, a rational parameter `t`, and two twists of

\[
E_\ell:y^2=x(x-\ell)(x+3\ell).
\]

The substitution `U=b(1−dt²)/(1+dt²)` and `H′=b′(b′−U²)` arranges that an odd negative valuation of `b` forces an odd valuation of `H′`. One of the paired twists then has even twist valuation. After scaling to a unit twist, a rational point with the prescribed odd `x` square-class is impossible by the valuation of its curve equation. This proves the formula's pole exclusion.

Completeness for integers requires actual rational points in specified square-classes. Weak approximation and finite multiplier catalogs first arrange real and local conditions. The squarefree classes `β,θ` have disjoint supports, and the author constructs a twist parameter `n` for which **both** `E_n` and `E_{nθ}` realize the needed class.

The two-descent analysis writes local conditions as binary matrices built from Legendre symbols. Its criterion is `ker K=⟨1,1_B⟩` together with `λ∉im K`. The resulting 2-Selmer group has dimension three; torsion contributes two dimensions, and a specific remaining class is exhibited locally everywhere. A rank-plus-Sha calculation bounds the **full** two-primary Selmer corank by one before any finiteness assumption is used.

At this point the argument imports the companion **pointwise 2-converse for elliptic curves with rational two-torsion**. Its finiteness conclusion, followed by the alternating Cassels–Tate pairing on the finite group, forces the residual 2-torsion dimension of Sha to be even. Hence the exhibited Selmer class comes from a rational point. The paper does not assume that the pairing restricted to `Sha[2]` is itself perfect.

For simultaneous twists, auxiliary prime coordinates create an invertible block matrix, while free diagonal choices handle the added support of `θ`. A multiaffine determinant argument chooses those bits. CRT and Dirichlet's theorem realize the finitely prescribed residue symbols. This is a constructed simultaneous condition, not an appeal to generic independence of all Legendre symbols.

## 4. From tested branches to actual valuations (§2.5–2.6)

A prime in a slope's odd-contact radical yields a contact value with odd valuation. After excluding fixed multiplier primes, it corresponds to one of the tested auxiliary elements. However, the ring `R` need not lie inside the original ambient valuation ring. Simply identifying a tested localization with that valuation ring would skip a necessary step.

The proof instead forms the convex subgroup of the nonstandard value group generated by negative valuations of ring elements. The pole-parity condition implies that valuations lying in this subgroup are even. An odd contact valuation therefore remains positive after coarsening. The coarsened valuation is nonnegative on the ring, and its center selects one of the two branches produced by the splitting of `sqrt(2)`.

Membership in the tested localized prime powers then gives the required ordinary precision in the original valuation. If the convex subgroup is nonzero, it contains every ordinary integer, so positivity in the quotient provides still stronger separation. The selected tested points are actual formal points. This completes the passage to `a≡η(a)` at every relevant contact prime.

## 5. The five-point height theorem (§4)

For one fixed set `C` of five rational points of `P¹`, including infinity, the author proves

\[
h(s)\le H\,M_S(s)^c.
\]

Here `h` is **logarithmic** absolute Weil height, and `M_S(s)` is the product, outside a fixed finite exceptional set, of primes having positive odd normalized contact depth with `C`. Primitive integral contact forms define those depths independently of the chosen projective coordinates. Enlarging the exceptional set changes the constant `H`; a fixed exponent `c` remains available.

### Fixed arithmetic geometry

The quaternion algebra of discriminant `210` gives a compact Shimura curve `X` of genus five. Its Atkin–Lehner group is `(Z/2)^4`. The quotient has genus zero and exactly five branch values: fixed points with labels `30,42,70,105,210`, together with Riemann–Hurwitz, force this count. Uniqueness of each labeled branch value makes it rational. Earlier tables and calculations of this quotient are acknowledged; the author uses the particular rational branch configuration.

A finite étale cover above `X` carries a principally polarized family of abelian surfaces with rational quaternionic multiplication. The map to `P¹` has ramification index two precisely at the five branch values. Over a bounded-degree splitting field of a rational fiber, even contact depth produces unramified lifts outside fixed bad primes. Odd-contact primes account for the variable ramification support. The family itself retains good reduction away from the fixed bad set after the necessary extension.

### Comparing heights and controlling fields

A nonconstant theta-null map on a finite level cover compares the base height with the stable Faltings height of the fiber. The proof handles choices of theta data, translations, finite coordinate transformations, the nonconstancy issue, and the original-versus-Deligne height normalization. The cited quantitative isogeny bound is later applied from a source whose height has already been bounded.

Defining all relevant geometric homomorphisms requires a bounded-degree extension `L`. Its degree is controlled through finite Galois images in bounded-rank integral linear groups, rather than through the eventual modular coefficient field. Quaternionic endomorphisms split the analysis:

- **CM fibers:** the surface is isogenous to the square of a CM elliptic curve. Bounded field degree bounds class number, leaving finitely many relevant CM data. The resulting constants need not be effective.
- **Non-CM fibers:** geometric endomorphism algebra is the quaternion division algebra. B-linear quasi-isogenies among conjugates yield a rational scalar cocycle. Dividing by fourth roots of degrees reduces it to a sign cocycle.

A Brauer-group/character construction splits the sign obstruction with a character whose order is bounded polynomially by the radical `M`. Unwanted ramification is removed by a quadratic twist. This produces a further field `L′` with degree `O(M)`; the earlier field `L` remains uniformly bounded. Confusing the two field degrees would lose the stated conductor control.

### Modularity, conductor and final height estimate

The modified two-dimensional representations are irreducible, odd and de Rham with the two required distinct Hodge–Tate weights. The paper imports the companion **Fontaine–Mazur modularity at the prime 2**, specifically without a residual-image restriction. The representation normalization fixes the weight and excludes an unintended Tate twist. Compatibility with the 3-adic realization is traced through algebraic traces of reduced quasi-endomorphisms, allowing the conductor at 2 to be treated using a different coefficient prime.

Large variable bad primes are tame, with conductor exponent at most two. The finitely many small primes have bounded ramification after finite extensions of bounded degree. The level therefore satisfies a polynomial bound, stated in the construction as `N≤CM²` up to fixed exceptions.

The abelian surface occurs as a factor of a suitable modular Jacobian. Belyi-degree and genus bounds control that Jacobian's stable height. A principally polarized complement is obtained using Zarhin's trick, and the quantitative isogeny estimate is applied **from powers of the bounded-height Jacobian**. A lower height bound for the complement then controls the desired surface. Combining this with the theta/base comparison gives `h(s)≤HM^c`.

## 6. Rank-one curve and finite formal logarithms (§5)

For `F=Q(sqrt(2))`, the ring of integers is a PID, and totally positive units are squares. The element `d` has norm seven. Two-isogeny descent on `E` and its partner gives descent-image sizes eight and one. Torsion accounts for four classes on `E`, while a point whose `x` coordinate has mixed real signs supplies an extra class. On the isogenous curve, valuation and positivity conditions leave only the square class. The descent formula yields rank one.

For the formal parameter, the coefficient recursion and the valuation of integrated coefficients make the logarithm truncation effective. At an unramified good prime `p>K+1`, the omitted tail has at least `K` extra valuation relative to the leading parameter. The quotient comparison requires the ratio of indices to be integral. The proof retains zero-index conventions and nonzero denominator conditions. These finite-polynomial statements are exactly what the ring-language tests can use.

## 7. Prime patterns and the order of limits (§6)

Every integer `A` is represented as three slopes `u_i/v`, with `u_3=Av−u_1−u_2`. The common denominator and the four other contact forms at each slope give thirteen linear forms. The desired values are signed fixed multipliers times large positive primes. The multiplier catalog is finite and chosen **before** `A` and the desired prime threshold.

Primes satisfy `r≡1 (mod 8)` and `r≡3 (mod 7)`. They split in `F`; the two conjugate elliptic reductions have opposite traces and orders `r+1±2a`, where `a²+b²=r`. Their common 2-part is four. An odd common divisor `l` forces `a≡0 (mod l)` and `b²≡−1 (mod l)`.

Fixed small-prime residue choices and extra multiplier primes make the divided forms integral, prime-admissible and in the desired classes. After fixing `A` and a real box, a cutoff `L` excludes all small odd common factors. Green–Tao–Ziegler, including the cited 2024 erratum, supplies a lower bound of order

\[
c_A(\log L)^{-C_0}\frac{X^3}{(\log X)^{13}}.
\]

Here `A,L` and every coefficient are fixed before `X→∞`. No uniform error estimate in those parameters is claimed or required.

To remove large common factors, the proof combines two upper bounds. A two-dimensional Selberg sieve counts bad prime values by

\[
O_A\!\left(\frac{X}{L\log X}+X^{7/8}+\sqrt X\log X\right).
\]

A second sieve on each affine fiber bounds the remaining prime-pattern completions by `O_A(X²/(log X)¹²)`, uniformly in the fiber. The nonzero fiber constant ensures that the restricted zero lines are distinct even when three ambient forms are linearly dependent. The displayed sieve weights give the explicit error `O(nZ⁶+Z⁸)` with `Z=X^(1/100)`.

The total loss is therefore `O_A(X³/[L(log X)¹³])` plus a smaller term. Choose `L` so that `1/L` beats the lower bound's logarithmic loss, **then** choose `X` large. A surviving prime pattern has conjugate reduction orders with gcd dividing four and prime-to-`r` orders. CRT supplies `h≡0 (mod d_1)` and `h≡4 (mod d_2r^K)`, explaining the paired local tests used earlier.

## 8. One polynomial error and the height contradiction (§2.7)

For a root tuple in the compactness model, put

\[
\delta=f(\eta(a_1),\ldots,\eta(a_n)),\qquad
B=\max(1,|\eta(a_1)|,\ldots,|\eta(a_n)|).
\]

If `δ=0`, elementarity gives an ordinary integer root. Otherwise `|δ|≤C_fB^D`, with `D=max(1,deg f)`. Choose a single ordinary precision `K≥cD`.

For **every** ring element `A`, take its three-slope representation at that same precision. At every prime in any selected slope radical, the paired local tests compare the fixed root coordinates with their indices. Polynomial evaluation then gives divisibility by `q^K` of the **same** nonzero `δ`. Consequently `M(s_i)^K` divides `δ` for every chosen slope, and

\[
h(\widetilde A)\le\sum_{i=1}^3 h(s_i)+\log3
\ll_f |\delta|^{c/K}+1\ll_f B.
\]

The implicit constant is ordinary and independent of `A` and the root tuple. The actual figure emphasizes this common-error uniformity. Allowing a different error for each slope would not establish this bound.

Apply it to the ring elements presenting the `x` coordinates of `T_{a_j}`. Canonical elliptic height grows quadratically in `η(a_j)`, giving `B²≪B`. Thus all the relevant nonstandard integer indices lie in an ordinary bounded interval and are ordinary integers. Additivity and injectivity identify the ring root with an ordinary integer tuple. This completes the finite-test implication.

## 9. Degree and normal-form consequences (§7)

Integer solvability has degree `0′`, and rational solvability is computably enumerable. Combined with the constructed oracle reduction, the latter has degree `0′` as claimed.

An arithmetic circuit for an arbitrary polynomial introduces a variable at each gate and equations of degree at most two. Over `Q`, their conjunction is equivalent to the vanishing of their squared sum, of degree at most four. Hence the same degree holds both for quartic inputs and for a supplied finite list of quadratics whose squared sum is tested.

The number of variables, number of quadratic summands and coefficient heights remain unbounded. The many-one normalization starts from **rational** solvability; it does not upgrade the integer-to-rational Turing reduction to a many-one reduction. No many-one completeness claim, uniform finite test bound or practical decision algorithm follows.
