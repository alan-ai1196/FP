## Question and principal result

The paper asks whether the asymptotic spectrum of tensors admits mutually compatible numerical prices whose elementary wire distances are exactly Euclidean. A price must respect all local tensor restrictions, tensor products and direct sums, not merely assign plausible costs to selected wiring diagrams.

Theorem 1.1 answers this over every coefficient field and on every real Hilbert space K. For a tensor T with its legs placed at x=(x1,...,xr), there is a compatible price H(T,x) with the following properties.

1. A binary identity wire between u and v has price ||u−v||.
2. A common q-valued label shared over a finite nonempty set S has price W(S)log2 q, where W is mean width normalized to give a segment its length.
3. For finite S of positive diameter, one spectral point F_S prices every tensor on S through H(T,x)=W(S)log2 F_S(T). Adding unused one-dimensional legs or grouping coincident legs preserves the price.
4. For nonzero T, the chosen price depends only on the labeled interpoint distances and is homogeneous under dilation. Joint convexity also makes it a seminorm of the placement modulo common translation.

For a finite set S, choose a in its affine span and a standard Gaussian g in the finite-dimensional linear span of S−a. The normalization is

\[
W(S)=\sqrt{2\pi}\,\mathbb E\max_{x\in S}\langle g,x-a\rangle .
\]

It is independent of a and of an isometric embedding into a larger Euclidean space. A singleton has width zero. A planar polygon has width equal to half its perimeter. The shared bit on three sites therefore costs half the triangle perimeter. Three independent binary wires on its edges instead cost the whole perimeter. Figure 1 makes this difference explicit.

These are algebraic invariants. The theorem does not identify them with the smallest realizable physical communication cost, Shannon information, finite-state memory, observation counts or a statistical risk. Arbitrary local linear maps and contraction at one site are free in this price calculus.

### Why averaged cut ranks are insufficient

Averaging logarithms of bipartite flattening ranks across Euclidean cuts correctly reproduces wire lengths and the mean width of a diagonal tensor. It does not in general exponentiate to a spectral point, because additivity under direct sum can fail.

The introduction uses horizontal and vertical wires on a square. For two selected cuts their rank pairs are (2,1) and (1,2), so their geometric-mean ranks are both sqrt(2). The direct sum has rank pair (3,3) and geometric-mean rank 3, rather than 2sqrt(2). A proof using alternative summands needs the stronger spectral axioms.

The main existence theorem is not obtained by exhibiting a simple closed formula for every tensor price. Theorem 1.2 first establishes spectral Euclidean flats of every finite dimension. Section 6 then selects a compatible isometry-invariant system on K by concentration and compactness. Most of the paper proves the existence of arbitrarily large flats by contradiction.

## Price calculus and geodesic extension, §§2–3

A spectral point on a fixed set of parties is additive on direct sums, multiplicative on tensor products, monotone under local restriction, and normalized at zero and the scalar unit. A scaled logarithm gives a price. Nonzero tensors at a single site have price zero and the zero tensor has price minus infinity.

Write d(a,b) for the binary-wire price and δ(S) for the binary diagonal price. Wire prices form a pseudometric. The diameter of S is a lower bound for δ(S), while any spanning tree length is an upper bound. Moving a leg of dimension q from x to y changes its price by at most d(x,y)log2 q. Summing over legs gives the transport estimate used repeatedly in limits and geometric comparisons.

For tensors with a common placement S and δ(S)>0, the ordinary sum satisfies

\[
H\!\left(\sum_i T_i\right)
\le \delta(S)\log_2\!\left(\sum_i2^{H(T_i)/\delta(S)}\right)
\le \max_i H(T_i)+\delta(S)\log_2(\#i).
\]

This is a bound for field-valued tensor sums, including cancellation. It is not a probability mixture. Tensor products add prices. Normalizing the restriction to a subset of parties requires a power of the spectral value. The proof that this power remains additive groups the terms in tensor powers and takes an asymptotic limit.

Fixed-format price sublevels are Zariski closed. The argument bounds the dimension spanned by nth powers by a binomial coefficient, whose logarithm divided by n vanishes. This closedness supports degeneration arguments. It is algebraic closedness, not a guarantee of small Euclidean-norm approximation error.

The strict comparison lemma says that if every spectral point strictly orders X below Y, a fixed number of copies of Y to the mth tensor power supplies X to the mth power for every m. Its proof uses a localized cone, an extremal positive state, and catalyst reuse. The lemma is the bridge from inequalities valid for all spectral prices to exact tensor identities.

The extension constructions preserve all prices of old tensors while inserting new sites. A point can be inserted between two sites at any prescribed division of their wire distance. The weighted-star construction places a center whose weighted distances attain the corresponding asymptotic tensor-price bound. The center may depend on the width profile.

The joint convexity proof uses exterior-algebra operators satisfying D_j L_i+L_i D_j=δ_ij I over the integers, including after specialization to characteristic two. Degree-level dimensions provide opposite logarithmic slopes. A coupled endpoint decomposition and successive refinement of coordinate ranges handle arbitrary couplings of endpoint choices, rather than only independent interpolation. Auxiliary-wire contributions cancel and the finite sum overhead vanishes per tensor power.

Repeated midpoint insertion and completion produce uniquely geodesic spectral spaces with convex distance between synchronized geodesics. Pointed ultralimits preserve the compatible prices. The construction is formulated to handle all tensors simultaneously, including over uncountable fields.

## Crossing identities force nonpositive curvature, §4

The shifted crossing theorem concerns two independent wires of logarithmic dimensions nt and nu. With S=t²+u² and −S<δ<S, their product has an ordinary two-term decomposition. The two summands factor across different crossing cuts with logarithmic ranks bounded by n sqrt(S+δ)+o(n) and n sqrt(S−δ)+o(n).

The included proof constructs these identities integrally. It uses exterior basis states indexed by finite sets of nonnegative modes, with energy equal to the sum of mode index plus one half. Normal-ordered free-fermion fields, integral divided derivatives and their commutation relation supply a pairing. A coefficient shift changes the two intermediate energy budgets in opposite directions.

The pairing blocks are unimodular through compound Pascal matrices on an energy downset, so inversion introduces no forbidden field denominator. The number D(H) of states below energy H satisfies log2 D(H) asymptotic to c sqrt(H). The proof obtains this from the partition product, independent occupations under an exponential weight, Riemann sums and a variance estimate. Choosing the two energy budgets and shift gives the advertised square-root exponents. Figures 3 and the original layouts on pp.17–19 specify the two cuts and the intermediate state count.

If a normed plane could occur locally as a spectral wire metric while violating the parallelogram law, the two crossing factorizations could both be made cheaper than the wire product. Spectral subadditivity rules this out. The source first treats differentiability points of the norm and then uses convexity of the dual ball to obtain an inner-product norm.

For complete geodesic spectral spaces, tangent and ultralimit arguments then give the CAT(0) squared-midpoint inequality

\[
d(z,m)^2\le \tfrac12d(z,x)^2+\tfrac12d(z,y)^2-\tfrac14d(x,y)^2 .
\]

The resulting four-point inequality compares the squared diagonals of a quadrilateral with its four squared sides. Equality gives flat rigidity. Euclidean cube corners can be filled by successive geodesic interpolation. These facts later turn a largest possible flat dimension into a uniform exclusion of a larger cube.

## A shared label has exactly mean-width price, §§5–6

The copying proposition is sharper than paying for a fresh wire from an arbitrarily chosen existing copy. If T already carries one common q-valued basis label on I, appending another copy at z increases its price by at most

\[
\bigl(\delta(I\cup\{z\})-\delta(I)\bigr)\log_2q.
\]

The proof uses an auxiliary diagonal, integer coefficient extraction, and a degeneration retaining the intended equal labels and an independent diagonal on the old sites. Taking tensor powers removes a fixed overhead. Field-extension invariance allows interpolation without changing the original-field prices.

This yields submodularity of δ on intersecting sets. A Behrend progression-free restriction of the triangle graph tensor gives twice the triangle diagonal price at most its perimeter. Consequently the copying increment into a triangle is bounded by half the detour length.

Theorem 5.3 proves a stronger uniqueness statement than the eventual choice of H. On any spectral Euclidean flat, every compatible price system gives δ(S)=W(S) for every finite nonempty S. The proof has several distinct steps.

- Joint convexity implies that adding a site inside the convex hull costs no extra diagonal price.
- Homogenized limits make δ translation invariant, Minkowski subadditive and Hausdorff continuous.
- Segment addition has exactly its length as the increment. The triangle identity involving T and −T then fixes the increment for a triangle.
- Linear combinations of triangle support functions are dense in the continuous functions on the sphere. The source proves the required density through an annihilating measure, hemisphere tests and Gaussian moments.
- These increments determine every polytope. The two homogeneous endpoint limits agree, forcing the same formula for the original prices.

Thus adding a copy of a label anywhere in the convex hull of its current copies has zero additional algebraic price. This statement presupposes that those indices already carry the same basis label. It does not say an unknown common latent variable can be reconstructed there for free.

Section 6 obtains a jointly isometry-invariant system from finite-dimensional flats. A Gaussian embedding divided by sqrt(N) makes the finitely many selected tensor prices concentrate about congruence-invariant expectations. A polar correction makes the embedding exactly isometric. A directed compactness limit over all finite comparison families gives one system on an arbitrary Hilbert space.

For a nonzero fixed T, placement price is a seminorm modulo translation. Every bounded linear operator L on K obeys

\[
H(T,Lx)\le \|L\|H(T,x).
\]

The section also identifies sqrt(t²+u²) as the sharp common crossing exponent, including algebraic border decompositions. This is a consequence of the price geometry, distinct from a numerical stability estimate.

## The contradictory hypothesis and borrowed wires, §§7–8

If the flat theorem failed over any field, field transfer would give a finite field F with a largest realized flat dimension k≥1. Transfer in positive characteristic starts from the prime field. Characteristic zero uses compatible rational tensors reduced modulo primes avoiding their denominators, followed by a limit. The two-party rank construction supplies a spectral line.

An approximate sequence of Euclidean (k+1)-cube corners would have an exact ultralimit. Flat filling and dilation would then produce a larger flat. The maximality assumption therefore gives a uniform cube exclusion over all spectral placements and scales.

The remaining construction works under this assumption, which will be contradicted. Its conditional algorithmic compressions must not be quoted as unconditional speedups.

A bit network has binary wires and trivalent local tensors. Size counts vertices and wires with multiplicity, including open ports. Fan-out requires equality vertices and is charged. A deterministic verification circuit is interpreted as a relation tensor with one accepting witness for each valid input. Most existence statements permit nonuniform choices unless the source explicitly supplies uniform control.

The tagged-permutation tensor Tag(h,w), with n=2^h records, checks a proposed list of tags and payloads against a prescribed payload list. The tags must form a permutation and each payload must equal its indicated source. For w=h it exposes 3nh bits.

An approximation X to f uses borrowed identity wires between opposite unit-frame sites. If their through price is h_X, a gap D means

\[
H(X-f\otimes I_X)\le h_X-D
\]

in every allowed ordered placement of the k-dimensional frame. This is an algebraic spectral-price discount of the error tensor. It is not a norm error, failure probability, prediction loss or guarantee that a practical noisy catalyst returns unchanged.

Data indices must remain exposed for arbitrary later contractions. A bound proved only after fixing p pins loses up to 2kp when all pin assignments are exposed. Substituting m approximations into a contraction loses at most 2km in the absolute gap after expansion over error subsets. These qualifications matter in the later simulation argument.

## Routing and the logarithmic-width tagged test, §§9–10

Under maximal flat dimension, Theorem 9.2 builds an exact permutation-routing identity with O(nw) catalyst bits. It is an ordinary sum over accepted selectors. Local tensors have fixed trivalent implementations, and a deterministic advised RAM with O(h)-bit words computes settings in O(n polylog h) word steps.

For each accepted term, a link of normalized width y, length charge λ and address scale t pays λy+t^p, where p>1 is fixed. The total obeys

\[
\sum_{\rm links}(\lambda y+t^p)\le 10n+2MN_c/w.
\]

The catalyst part pays for their return across opposite layers. At positive scale a slot has at most 2^(th+o(h)) possible partners. Width and slot fees are accounted separately.

### Adaptive probing

Outstanding commodity widths define an inventory z. A strongly convex score f defines

\[
F_s(z)=\max_{0\le x\le a}\{\langle z,x\rangle-sf(x)\}.
\]

The maximizer gives marginal prices for demands. Its lower-face condition ensures positive price only when enough inventory is present. Excess stock can always be fulfilled along a fixed bounded-hop core route.

A local test asks whether one spectral price system realizes the quoted commodity prices, fixed catalyst distances and upper bounds on available links in a bounded ball. A failed test gives a cheaper exact replacement identity through strict spectral comparison and compactness. Fixed finite menus and histogram selectors control the description cost and retain the original field-valued multiplicities.

When the core test passes but the score is high, an activation buys probes. The change forced in the price array has a Bregman divergence large enough to pay their capacity fee. A reserve-and-feed argument for the convex potential turns this surplus into a bounded number of purchases and local replacement rounds.

### Local coordinates and signed displacement

The routing layout subdivides a stage of scale t into 18(d−1)+1 stages of scale t/d, with O(n) advised word work for fixed d. Sparse samplers join sufficiently large sets of tile endpoints.

An altimeter is the distance from one tile's center to a far endpoint of the next column, minus M. Under consistent local tests, long catalyst axes become parallel lines in a CAT(0) ultralimit. Repeated parallel-set splitting puts all axes into R^k times another factor. Cube exclusion forces that last factor to be a point.

A finite-segment tail bound then identifies each altimeter's positive part with the positive signed displacement in a local Euclidean frame. Different test balls may use different frames. The fixed layer placement used to price a routed tensor is a different construction, as Figure 4 emphasizes.

A high convex score marks many tiles with a common signed direction. Along a chain, signed displacements add across zero-length joins. A unit-length probe between its endpoints forces at least one marked reading to fall. The squared-positive-part score produces the required divergence. The p>1 fee decays fast enough at small fixed address scale to pay for the probes. Figure 5 shows the chain and endpoint constraint.

### From wide payloads to width h

Discarding terms that spend too little on crossing layers creates a gap of order nw. For w at least h^(1+κ), that gap dominates the O(nh polylog h) cost of summing over tags and selectors. Each retained term has positive-scale traffic budget at most 11n.

The compaction lemma sends any active set occupying at most half a target array injectively along a fixed bounded-degree graph. It also supplies a deterministic linear-word-time assignment with advice. This bounds installed physical slots, including idle ones, rather than merely counting active links in one term.

Sparse exact corrections amplify a batch of s tagged-test approximations. Truncating the error expansion at floor(s/h) errors gives gap at least sD/(2h), subject to the stated D and log(s) bounds. Compaction limits how many exact tagged-test modules must be installed. Unique trace completions and canonical padding avoid accidental multiplicity changes over F.

The width profile r(a) measures overhead at payload width h^a above the port count. Stable radix packing gives

\[
r(1)\le 1-\frac1a+\max\{r(a)/a,\inf_{b\ge1}r(b)\}.
\]

The routing recursion makes every suitable decreasing positive comparison profile an upper bound for r(a). Choosing such a profile contradicts any positive r(1). Theorem 10.1 therefore gives, for every r>0, an exposed approximation to Tag(h,h) of size O(2^h h^(1+r)) and gap at least 2^h 2^(−C(log2(2h))²). This conclusion remains under the maximal-flat contradiction hypothesis.

## Exact network compression, §§11–14

The second branch of the proof converts cube exclusion into bounded-degree port graphs. Their uniform nonlinear Poincaré inequality is

\[
\frac1{|V|^2}\sum_{i,j}d(x_i,x_j)^2
\le \gamma\,\frac1{|V|}\sum_{i,j}G_{ij}d(x_i,x_j)^2
\]

for every spectral placement over F.

On a finite binary Grassmannian, the four-point inequality bounds average squared distance of complementary subspaces by m times that of adjacent subspaces. Equality would realize the excluded cube. Compactness makes the improvement uniform. Recursive sparse matrices grow in order faster than their Poincaré constants, and a balanced replacement construction plus a CAT(0) barycenter estimate produces a family of fixed degree and uniform bound. The source includes the construction, rather than assuming all CAT(0) spaces admit the required expanders.

### Turning metric inequalities into exact identities

Theorem 12.1 takes a fixed demand motif D and connected supply motif S. If every spectral wire metric satisfies

\[
\sum_{e\in D}p_e d_e^2\le H\sum_{e\in S}q_e d_e^2,
\]

then for every H'>H the demand wires have an ordinary sum of local images of supply wires, with

\[
\sum_{e\in S}c_e^2/q_e\le H'\sum_{e\in D}b_e^2/p_e+O(1).
\]

The proof uses finite rational direction nets, strict spectral comparison and a small selector fraction. Grouping sequences by their total supply payload gives compact outer indices. Finitely many base-map parameters satisfy integer polynomial equations. Once these equations are satisfied, the resulting identities hold over every commutative unital ring. This is not a claim that a suitable assignment exists over every ring.

For the six-party motif in Figure 6, demand masses are 1/3 and 1. The two supply groups each have total mass 4/3. A CAT(0) metric inequality gives H=31/16. Choosing H'<2 and then one of two consistent regroupings discards at least half the supply energy. This leaves a strict leading saving H'/2<1.

### Iteration and accounting

The network has a fixed regular base graph and binary lane coordinates. Each actual arc has an integer bit width. With uniform arc mass, the quadratic width energy controls total width by Cauchy–Schwarz.

The packing construction marks thick and thin endpoints, concatenates thick–thick wires, assigns the remaining demands, and moves every whole old vertex consistently even when motifs overlap. Integer slicing and degree regularization add O(P²) to the energy. Several shrinking steps reduce its leading coefficient. The port graph then restores bounded degree with a multiplicative loss independent of the intermediate degree.

One block gives L_(i+1)≤L_i/4+cP_i² while the number of positions shrinks geometrically. After T blocks,

\[
\sum_{\rm final\ wires}b_e\le\sqrt{L_T}
\le 2^{-T}\bigl(\sqrt{L_0}+O(P_0)\bigr).
\]

Each local contraction has at most C2^T constituent occurrences and field lengths at most C2^T(W0+1). Numerical descriptors are constructed in O(P0 polylog n) nondeterministic RAM work, with successful paths in bijection with admissible identity-term choices. These bounds pay for descriptions and hidden contractions instead of equating reduced wire width with an immediately efficient algorithm.

Section 14 compiles many copies of a normalized trivalent network G using tagged-permutation gates of one length. For N=2^h≤m≤N^J, size(G)≤m and e open bits, it constructs G to the dth tensor power with size

\[
O\bigl(d(m/h^a+e\,\operatorname{polylog}h)\bigr)
\]

for fixed a>0 and log2 d=O((log h)²). Every incident bit at a tagged gate is charged. Length lifting supplies short sorts. Stacked copies share long wiring names, local programs have short keys, streams are matched by sorting, and a finite-field lookup table evaluates local contraction records. Exposed-port costs remain explicit.

## Amortized simulation and the final contradiction, §§15–16

Under maximal flat dimension, Theorem 15.1 repeatedly combines exact descent with the approximate tagged test. For a sufficiently large fixed A it chooses A'>A. A tick of size V=2^w and at most V/w^A' pins has, for some q≤V^o(1), an approximation to q independent copies of size at most qV/w^A and pointwise gap V^(1−o(1)).

Replacing tagged gates introduces catalyst ports. Reusing each catalyst along a line of a k-dimensional grid exposes only the two ends of that line. The lens estimate bounds the extra price from the sloping endpoint links. For E external rails, grid side g, foci at distance R and at most 2^ℓ choices, the error price is at most

\[
2RE-D+O_k(Eg^2/R)+2kR(\ell+g^k).
\]

The exact binomial form in Lemma 15.2 retains the number of erroneous cells. Choosing a sufficiently distant focus and then rescaling gives gap V^(1−o(1)) while reducing external catalyst pairs per copy by a factor g. Figure 7 shows why only the endpoint links create a geometric overhead.

The final diagonal argument uses universal weighted RAM ticks over the fixed finite field. A program a controls how external wire pairs attach to the grid, without seeing their bit values. Z(a) denotes the exact tensor and Z-hat(a) its accelerated version. The gap, rail and selector budgets guarantee

\[
H(Z(a)-\widehat Z(a))<H(I)=2RE.
\]

The accelerator X is selected before the controller a. An entry evaluator for Z-hat treats a as data. Its weighted computation sums accepting terminal weights, so this is an exact field-valued evaluator, not an approximate probabilistic simulator.

The trace-distribution lemma applies the exact compressor to this evaluator. It keeps both bits of each external pair together. Histories and numerical descriptors determine input assignments independently of the input values. There are at most V^0.6 factors and description bits, each factor has bounded work and input size, and the sum of work charges is at most U times a fixed power of w. The shared final-wire string shrinks by 2^−s.

The controller is then programmed with one identity branch and branches that distribute the accelerated evaluator's trace over the exact ticks. Resource grouping keeps work and pin loads within the separately fixed budgets. Canonical encodings give exactly one selector for each history and final-wire assignment. The controller can supply its own bytes as data because the universal machine and trace layout were fixed before those values.

Entrywise this yields Z(a)−Z-hat(a)=I, contradicting the strict price inequality. Thus no finite field has a largest spectral flat dimension. Field transfer gives every finite dimension over every field, and the construction in Section 6 completes Theorem 1.1.

The compression, routing and simulation assertions used inside this contradiction are not independent unconditional consequences for ordinary computation. Their strongest forms assume precisely the maximal-flat condition that the argument disproves.
